# Generated Story Engagement Contract

This contract applies to future Story AI output. It does not require the deterministic provider-free closed-beta fallback to become the final product-quality story experience.

The deterministic fallback exists first as a safe, reliable demo and recovery path. When Story AI is deliberately enabled later, generated stories must be noticeably more engaging than that fallback while preserving the same safety, privacy, localization, memory and cost boundaries.

## Narrative roles

QISSA is a narrated story with an in-world hero. The child is the listener/reader and decision-maker, not automatically a character inside the prose.

- The narrator tells the story.
- The selected hero is the in-world protagonist.
- The child participates at explicit choice moments by deciding what the hero should do.
- The world remembers the selected choice and reflects it later through consequences, relationships, recurring characters and canon.

Ordinary story prose must not continuously address the child as `you` or place the listener inside the scene unless a future explicit child-as-hero mode is separately designed and approved.

Personalization should come from the selected hero, world, recurring characters, remembered choices and consequences rather than simulated immersion of the listener.

## What the child should feel

For ages 5–7, bedtime does not mean uneventful. The story should be calm enough to read before sleep, but it should still make the child want to know what happens next.

A strong story should create one clear form of curiosity early:
- a funny or surprising event;
- a small mystery or question;
- a clear child-scale desire;
- a friend who needs help;
- an object, creature or place behaving in an unexpected but safe way.

The story should then keep paying that curiosity forward through action, dialogue, discovery and consequence until the ending slows down.

## Opening and orientation

Do not equate a strong opening with a context-free cold open.

For 5–7 bedtime stories:
- the first short paragraph should establish where the story is, who the hero is, and what the hero is doing;
- keep that orientation compact and concrete rather than spending a paragraph on decorative scenery;
- introduce the unusual event, desire, question or small problem within roughly the first 60–120 words;
- make the central story question understandable by roughly the first 100–120 words;
- avoid trailer-style sound effects or sudden action before the listener understands the scene unless the context itself already makes the opening clear.

The child should feel invited into a real story, not dropped into the middle of a game event.

## Description budget

Description is useful when it helps the child picture something that matters to the current action.

Avoid:
- three or more consecutive sentences of static scenery or explanation;
- lists of decorative details that do not affect what happens;
- repeatedly restating that the place is calm, beautiful, quiet, safe or magical;
- long narrator explanations of what a character feels when a visible reaction could show it instead.

Prefer one or two concrete sensory details, then move the scene forward.

## Scene momentum

After the brief setup, every one or two short paragraphs should contain a meaningful change. For example, somebody:
- acts;
- reacts;
- discovers something;
- asks or answers;
- makes a joke;
- tries something;
- makes a small mistake;
- notices a clue;
- decides what to do next.

The change does not need to be loud or exciting. A bedtime story can stay gentle while still moving.

## Character and dialogue

Dialogue and visible action should carry much of the middle of the story. Supporting characters should have distinct behavior rather than existing only to explain the lesson.

Use small, memorable traits such as:
- a character who always miscounts;
- a careful character paired with an impulsive one;
- a tiny character attempting an oversized task;
- a recurring joke or harmless habit that matters to the plot.

Do not turn the hero into an adult supervisor, project manager or moral lecturer.

## Anticipation and payoff

Keep one simple anticipation loop alive through the story. The child should have a reason to continue listening, such as wanting to know:
- whether the funny plan will work;
- what caused the unusual clue;
- where the strange light leads;
- how the hero will help a friend;
- what will happen after the child's selected choice.

Pay that loop off before the final bedtime coda. Do not replace the payoff with a lecture or a generic statement that everyone learned something.

## Choice consequence

The choice is a decision the child makes about the hero. It is not proof that the child is physically inside the story.

The story should pause at a natural decision point. The UI then presents two understandable actions the hero could take. Both may lead to the same broad safe ending, but the route, interactions, discoveries or remembered consequence should feel meaningfully different.

The bridge after confirmation stays short. Episode 2 continues after the bridge and spends most of its time showing the consequence rather than repeating the selected action.

## Memory and second-session wow

The strongest personalization signal is visible continuity. A previous child choice should later affect the world in a concrete way: a recurring character remembers what happened, an object returns, a relationship changes, or a later situation develops differently.

UI copy may say that the child's previous choice was remembered. Story prose should normally express the consequence inside the fictional world rather than address the listener directly.

## Bedtime ending

The final 10–15% may deliberately reduce energy. By then the main question or problem should already be solved.

The closing section can use softer sensory detail, shorter dialogue and a warm final image. Calmness belongs especially here, after the child has already received the story payoff.

## Release rule

Word-count, duration, safety and structural checks are necessary but cannot prove that a story is interesting. Before Story AI is enabled for real users, generated samples must also pass human editorial review for:
- clear orientation and natural opening;
- correct separation of narrator, hero and child roles;
- early curiosity without a context-free cold open;
- description density;
- scene momentum;
- dialogue/character life;
- meaningful choice consequence;
- visible remembered continuity;
- natural RU/UZ language;
- satisfying but calm closure.
